---I've been running small teams and digital agencies since 2001. I've watched the project management software market explode — dozens of tools, hundreds of features, billions in funding. I've tried most of them.

I keep coming back to Basecamp.

Not because I haven't explored the alternatives. I have. Asana, Monday, Notion, ClickUp, Jira, Trello — I've used them all seriously, some for years. And they're not bad tools. Some of them are genuinely impressive in specific ways.

But none of them think the way an agency thinks.

## Most PM Tools Are Built for the Wrong Mental Model

The dominant model in project management software is the **team-centric view**. You organize around people, departments, and workflows. You have boards and sprints and velocity charts. You assign seats, build org structures, and report up.

That's a fine model if you're a product company with stable teams working on a single product.

It's the wrong model entirely if you're running an agency.

An agency lives and dies by projects. Every engagement is a container — it has a client, a scope, a timeline, a set of deliverables, and a defined end. Your team composition shifts constantly. A freelancer joins for one project. A client needs to see progress without accessing anything else. A contractor handles three projects simultaneously with three different teams.

The work isn't a continuous stream. It's a series of ships you launch and complete.

Basecamp understood this from the beginning. A project in Basecamp is a real thing — a named, self-contained space with its own message board, to-dos, files, schedule, and chat. Everything about a client engagement lives inside that project. You invite who needs to be there. You close it when it's done.

That sounds obvious. It isn't. Most tools treat projects as filtered views of a global task list. Basecamp treats them as actual places.

## What 23 Years Teaches You About Client Work

When you've been doing this long enough, you stop looking for the perfect tool and start looking for the one that causes the least friction.

Friction in client work looks like this: a client emails you because they couldn't figure out where to post their feedback. A new team member spends two days just learning where things are. A contractor who was on three projects now has access to your entire workspace because the tool doesn't think in terms of project-level permissions.

Basecamp has always had a clear answer to these problems. Clients get a project. They see what's relevant to them. They don't see your other clients. They can post messages, respond to questions, upload files — without needing a manual or a training call.

That's not a small thing. That's hours saved per project, multiplied across every project you run per year.

The message board format especially — which most people dismiss as old-fashioned — is actually exactly right for async client communication. Threaded email-style discussions with context, not a firehose chat room where decisions get buried.

I've onboarded clients who are not technical at all and watched them navigate Basecamp without help. That almost never happens with other tools.

## It's Not Perfect

I'm not writing a Basecamp ad.

There are two real problems I keep running into, and they matter.

The first is **onboarding friction for new users and clients**. Basecamp is simpler than most tools, but it has accumulated features over the years, and some of the UI patterns are genuinely hard to discover. New clients occasionally get confused about where to post something, or don't realize a feature exists. The learning curve isn't steep, but it's there, and with clients you often get one shot to make the experience feel effortless.

The second is **seat pricing at scale**. Basecamp's flat-rate model ($299/month for unlimited users) is actually generous once you cross a certain team size. But for smaller agencies running many projects with rotating freelancers and per-project client access, the calculus gets uncomfortable. You start making decisions about who to invite based on cost, not based on who actually needs to be there. That's a wrong incentive.

These aren't dealbreakers. I've worked around both for years. But they've been sitting in the back of my head for a long time.

## Something Is Forming

I've been thinking about what a tool built on Basecamp's philosophy — but designed around how a modern small agency actually operates — would look like.

Not a competitor, exactly. More like a response to the same problem, with a different set of tradeoffs.

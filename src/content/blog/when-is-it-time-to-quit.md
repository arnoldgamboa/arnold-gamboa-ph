There is a kind of quitting that feels like failure before you say the word.

Maybe it is a business you have carried for years. It still has a name, a website, a few customers, and enough activity to make next month feel possible. But it is not growing. You are keeping it alive with energy that should go somewhere else.

Maybe it is smaller than that. I have kept a domain name for decades, even though I no longer use it. Letting it go should be easy. But it is tied to an earlier version of my work and hopes. A practical choice becomes personal.

The same thing can happen in church planting. A planter can be tired, under-resourced, dealing with people and process problems, and carrying an emotional and spiritual load. There may be no growth or handoff. At what point do you call it a good run instead of another hard season?

## The difference between a dip and a dead end

Seth Godin wrote about this in *The Dip*, a book about knowing when to quit and when to stick. His basic point is useful: some hard seasons are part of getting good at worthwhile work. Others are dead ends that keep taking without a path forward.

That distinction is harder in real life. We are not only looking at a spreadsheet. We are looking at time spent, people we care about, promises we made, and an identity we have worn.

Emotional attachment can make a dead end look like faithfulness. Pride can make a costly project look like perseverance. Fear can make a familiar loss feel safer than an uncertain future.

So I think the question is not simply, “Can I endure this?” People can often endure too much. The better question is, “What is this work producing, and what is it costing?”

If it is draining you emotionally, financially, and spiritually, that matters. If there is no learning, progress, honest path to health, or reason beyond attachment, quitting may be the most logical thing to do.

## Calling changes the calculation

Still, I do not think logic is the whole story.

Some work is costly because it is broken. Some work is costly because it is yours to do. Those are not the same thing.

Calling does not make bad strategy good, and it does not mean refusing help, feedback, rest, or a necessary ending. But if God has called you to a work, difficulty alone is not enough reason to walk away. Some seasons require continuing precisely because the work is hard.

That is why discernment matters more than stubbornness. We need people who tell us the truth, not just cheer us on. Are we holding on because God is leading us, or because we cannot release something that once mattered?

Sometimes faithfulness means staying through the dip. Sometimes it means ending well, grieving honestly, and making room for the next assignment.

Quitting is not always failure. Holding on is not always faith.

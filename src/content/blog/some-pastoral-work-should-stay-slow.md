I spend a lot of time looking for friction I can remove. That instinct makes sense when I am leading engineers or building software. Repeated steps should become a script. Slow handoffs should be fixed. A tool should earn its place by making useful work easier.

Pastoral work has made me suspicious of applying that instinct everywhere.

Some friction is waste. Some friction is where attention begins. The difficult part is telling them apart.

I feel this tension because I move between engineering, building, and ministry. In one part of my life, removing a step is progress. In another, that same step may be where I notice a person.

## Speed can hide what needs attention

AI can turn rough notes into a clean announcement, summarize a long document, or organize scattered sermon research. I am glad it can. I do not feel spiritually obligated to spend an hour formatting information that a machine can arrange in seconds.

But speed changes my posture when I stop noticing what the task was asking from me.

A church message may look like a writing problem while really being a people problem. A sermon outline may look unfinished because the structure is weak. It may also be unfinished because I have not sat with the text long enough. A crowded calendar may need automation. It may need me to admit that I said yes too often.

AI cannot make that distinction for me. It can only make the instruction I gave it easier to complete.

That is useful, but it is also dangerous in a quiet way. A fast answer can let me move past the moment when I should have stopped and paid closer attention.

## I want selective inconvenience

I am starting to think that a good pastoral tool should sometimes refuse to make the work feel finished.

It can clean up grammar while leaving the hard sentence for me. It can collect background material while asking which claim I am prepared to own. It can prepare a draft without turning the draft into an automatic send. The pause should not be decorative. It should return responsibility to the pastor.

This is different from making software deliberately annoying. Nobody needs another clumsy church system defended as a spiritual discipline. Administrative waste is still waste. If a task involves copying dates, resizing text, or chasing the same information across five places, I want the machine to help.

I am more careful when the work involves interpretation, care, or judgment. There, convenience should have a limit.

That limit will not be the same for every pastor or every church. I do not have a perfect rule for it. My current test is simple: if removing the friction also removes a moment of attention, I should pause before automating it.

I want technology to clear the clutter, then get out of the way. The work left behind needs my attention. I am learning not to resent the fact that some of it stays slow.

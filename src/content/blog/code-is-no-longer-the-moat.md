I had a call with an old client this week.

He owns a digital agency. He wanted my take on a potential project.

It didn't take long to see it. The things he was asking me to check, he can now do himself with Claude.

So I told him plainly. I can't offer the same things I did a couple of years ago.

## The wall came down

For a long time, code was the moat. If you could build it, people needed you. If you could read it, fix it, review it, they needed you even more.

That wall is mostly gone. Not completely. But enough that pretending otherwise would be dishonest, to clients and to myself.

So the question isn't whether things changed. They did. The question is what's still ours.

## What still holds, for now

I keep coming back to three things.

**Architecture.** Knowing how the pieces should fit before anyone writes a line. Knowing what will break at scale, what will be painful to change later, what looks fine in a demo and falls apart in real use. AI will probably take this too, eventually. But today, it's still a moat.

**Relationships.** Past clients know you. They trust you. And many of them are simply too busy to do it themselves, even if the tools make it possible. Being able to doesn't mean having the time. They'll still hand the work to someone they trust.

**Explaining things clearly.** Years in the field give you something a random vibe coder doesn't have yet: you can tell a client what's going on, why it matters, and what to do next, in words they understand. That sets you apart with the clients you have and the ones you haven't met.

None of these are about typing code. All of them are about judgment, trust, and clarity.

## Reinvent, not retreat

I'm not writing this to sound the alarm.

There's still hope for programmers and agency owners. But it won't look like what worked before.

The work was never really the code. We just got paid for it that way.

We have to reinvent ourselves in this weird AI era. Start with what still holds. Build from there.
